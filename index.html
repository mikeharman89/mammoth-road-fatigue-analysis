The Setup

What we tracked
and why it matters

Regular season stats tell you who scored. They don't tell you who was running on fumes when they did it.

We built something called the Road Trip Decay Index — a measure of how a player's scoring output changes between game 1 of a road trip and game 3 and beyond, when the cumulative grind of travel, hotel beds, and consecutive nights in different cities starts to show up in the numbers. We ran it across two full seasons of Utah data, then asked the obvious question: does it get better or worse over time?

The short answer is: it depends on who you're asking about. Three things stood out clearly enough to matter heading into the playoffs.

Takeaway 01

Cooley is
playoff-ready

His road fatigue was real. And then the injury happened, and something clicked.

For his first season and a half, Logan Cooley had a consistent pattern: elite on the first game of a road trip, noticeably worse by game three. His Road Trip Decay Index sat at −0.49 across 2024/25 — meaning he was producing roughly half a point per game less when trips got deep. Not a crisis, but a real, measurable pattern that showed up every time we looked at the data.

Then he got hurt in December. Missed nine weeks of team games, plus the Olympic break. Came back February 25th looking like a different player by the numbers. In his final 25 regular season games, his road game 1 output (1.00 pts/gm) matched his road game 3+ output exactly. Decay index: 0.00. That's not small sample noise — that's a pattern that held across an entire final stretch of the regular season.

Takeaway 02

The depth lines
transformed

Nobody's going to write a feature about Kevin Stenlund's road trip durability. But they probably should.

Here's something that doesn't make headlines: the players who grind through games 5, 6, and 7 of a playoff series on the road are often not the stars. They're the checking line, the third pair, the guys logging 12 minutes a night making sure the opponent doesn't get anything easy. And this year, Utah's depth became significantly better at holding up deep in road trips.

Stenlund's road trip decay went from −0.19 in 2024/25 to +0.28 in 2025/26 — a swing of +0.47, the biggest improvement on the roster. Crouse improved by +0.23. McBain got better too. These aren't coincidences. Utah's bottom six is a different unit on the road than it was a year ago.

Why this matters in the playoffs

A seven-game series on the road is a different beast from an 82-game schedule. Coaches lean on depth. If Utah finds themselves in a series that goes the distance, having a third line that holds up — rather than fading — could be the difference in a game 6 or 7.

Takeaway 03

Watch Guenther
and Keller

They're still two of the best players on the ice. The data just says don't take it for granted late in a road series.

This one's counterintuitive, because both Guenther and Keller had excellent seasons. But their road trip decay got measurably worse year-over-year — Guenther by −0.28, Keller by −0.23. The most likely explanation isn't that they got less durable; it's that Utah leaned on them harder down the stretch of a playoff push, and that load showed up in the data.

It's worth noting what this looks like in practice: Keller's road game 1 is still 0.73 pts/gm, and Guenther's is 0.85. These are still elite players. The question is whether the coaching staff manages their minutes smartly when deep into a road trip in April and May, or whether they keep leaning on them the same way they did all regular season.

The coaching implication

Neither Guenther nor Keller needs to be protected in a short series. But if Utah advances deep — second round, conference finals — games 5, 6, 7 on the road are where smart minutes management on their two stars could pay off.

Methodology

How we built this

All data pulled directly from the NHL's public API — no proprietary sources, no manual tracking. The Road Trip Decay Index compares each player's points per game in road game 1 against games 3 and beyond. A minimum of 3 qualifying deep road games was required for inclusion in any phase. Cooley's 2025/26 season was split into pre-injury, post-return, and late-season phases given the 82-day gap in his log (nine weeks injured + the three-week Winter Olympics break).

The full analysis covers 12 Utah skaters across both the 2024/25 and 2025/26 regular seasons. Points are an imperfect proxy for physical load — EDGE tracking data (speed, acceleration, distance) would strengthen these signals considerably, but remains inaccessible via the public API.
